6. ORGANIZING LARGE PROJECTS
Introduction
Managing a large project is a challenging endeavor that requires overcoming a confluence
of factors like the fast pace of technological advances, increased regulation, increased
competition that can come from any part of the world, volatility along the supply chain,
etc. Managing an IT project is compounded by the fact that technological advances affect
both the management of a project and its product—technology is used both as a tool and
a solution. To respond to these challenges, organizations sort their project work into hier-
archies with portfolio, program, and project levels, create centralized control structures
like dedicated project management offices, and adopt or modify existing project manage-
ment methodologies to accommodate their needs.
In large IT projects, the system under development is typically broken down into individ-
ual pieces that are assigned for further breakdown to individual teams and groups of
teams. These teams create their own medium-level designs that break down into smaller
pieces that are sent for coding. At that stage, more developers typically join the teams;
similarly, as tasks are completed, some developers leave the teams. This creates “fluid”
team structures that pose challenges for project managers in terms of cultivating the cul-
ture they want. In some projects, multiple pieces can be assigned to a single team, requir-
ing close and continuous cooperation of the team members (Martin et al., 2005a).
6.1 PMBOK Guide
The acceptance of project management as a profession signaled the need to formalize the
field with principles, knowledge, processes, skills, tools, best practices, techniques, and
the organization of the practitioners into professional societies and associations like the
Project Management Institute, Inc. (PMI). In addition to bringing professionals together,
the purpose of PMI (since becoming accredited as a standards developer by the American
National Standards Institute in 1998) is to develop standards and guideline publications to
advise and recommend best practices.
The outcome of one such an initiative was the publication of the Project Management
Body of Knowledge (PMBOK) which has become the de facto choice for project manage-
ment (especially for large projects) in North America and a preferred choice in many other
parts of the world. PMBOK describes the life cycle of a project and its management by con-
sidering globally recognized standards and guides that professional project managers
have identified as important for the successful execution of projects (Larson & Gray, 2015).
The first two sections of the guide provide an introduction to general project management
concepts while the remaining sections discuss the 47 logically grouped project manage-
ment processes that form the guide to the project management body of knowledge. These
sections typically follow a systems structure with inputs to a process, tools for accomplish-
ing the work, and outputs.

Although the process phases/groups are distinct with well-defined interfaces, it is
assumed that in practice they will overlap and interact as per the specifics of each project.
Their purpose as per the PMBOK is to guide the application of project management knowl-
edge and skills. In this sense, predictive and iterative approaches are welcomed, and how
to proceed is up to the project manager and the team. For example, monitoring and con-
trolling processes can take place parallel to other processes so they can be seen as a back-
ground group of processes in the project life cycle.
The other dimension along which processes are organized in PMBOK is the project knowl-
edge, which is grouped into ten areas. They represent a complete set of concepts, terms,
and activities that make up the professional field of project management. They are
generic enough to cover every aspect of project activity and each knowledge area is inte-
grated across the five process groups either as distinct or overlapping processes that inter-
act with each other depending on the project specifics.
The project integration management area includes six processes that span all process
groups. They identify, define, combine, unify, and coordinate the various processes and
project management activities within each group. In the project management context,
integration includes characteristics of unification, consolidation, communication, and
integrative actions that are crucial to controlled project execution through completion,
successfully managing stakeholder expectations, and meeting requirements. Project inte-
gration management includes making choices about resource allocation, making trade-
offs among competing objectives and alternatives, and managing the interdependencies
between the project management knowledge areas.
The project scope management area includes six processes that are spread between the
planning and the monitoring and controlling groups. They aim to ensure that only the
work that is required to complete the project successfully is included. Under project
scope, we include the work performed to deliver the solution and not necessarily the fea-
tures and functions that characterize the solution which is often referred to as “product
scope.” Completion of the former is measured against the project management plan; the
latter is measured against the product requirements.
The project time management area includes seven processes that are, like the project
scope, spread between the planning and the monitoring and controlling groups. These
processes ensure that the project will be completed in a timely manner by applying tools
and techniques to develop the project schedule including durations, dependencies, and
other planning information. In small projects, developing the project schedule and esti-
mating the required resources might be seen as a single process that can be performed by
one person in a relatively short amount of time. In PMBOK, these processes are considered
separate because the tools and techniques for each one of them are different.

The project cost management area includes four processes that also spread between the
planning and the monitoring and controlling groups. They address any aspects related to
project cost, including planning, estimating, budgeting, financing, funding, managing, and
controlling (to ensure the project is completed within the approved budget). Additionally,
the recurring costs of using, maintaining, and supporting the project solution will be
required in many instances. In small projects, these activities can be seen as a single activ-
ity that one person typically undertakes; in larger projects, they are seen as separate, pri-
marily because they require different skillsets, tools, and techniques. The stakeholders’
view of cost should be considered here as it might vary according to their interests—for
example, the cost of a resource can be different at the planning, acquisition, and delivery
times.
The project quality management knowledge area includes one process for each of the
planning, executing, and monitoring and controlling groups. It includes activities that
determine quality policies, objectives, and responsibilities to ensure the project solution
satisfies the quality standards set by the project stakeholders. Continuous process
improvement and quality standards are supported and established regardless of the
nature of the deliverables to avoid negative consequences for any or all of the project
stakeholders. For example, exhausting the team members to meet customer requirements
might keep the customer happy but could impact the organization negatively due to
employee attrition, errors, omissions, etc.
The human resource management area includes one process in the planning group and
three in the executing group. They discuss the organization and management of the
project team. Project teams pose many challenges because of their tentative nature (they
only come together for the duration of the project), their employment type (part-time or
full-time), their skill diversity, their career aspirations (the project subject might add value
to their experience), and their location (virtual or physical participation), among others.
Managing and leading the project team requires building trust and commitment, under-
standing organizational and national cultures, influencing the environment, and commu-
nicating the need to eliminate any factors that may alter project performance.
The communications management area includes one process for each of the planning,
executing, and monitoring and controlling groups. It includes activities that ensure timely
and appropriate planning, collection, creation, distribution, storage, retrieval, manage-
ment, control, monitoring, and the ultimate disposition of project information. Effective
communication creates transparency and brings stakeholders closer together despite
their potentially different backgrounds, levels of expertise, interests, and perspectives.
Their positive influence on the project and their alignment with its vision is vital, so com-
munication becomes one of the core activities of the project manager.
The risk management knowledge area has five processes in the planning group and one in
the monitoring and controlling group. It includes processes for conducting risk manage-
ment planning, identification, analysis, response planning, and controlling risk on a
project. Risk is inherent in projects and can be anticipated or unexpected. When some-
thing is a known risk, it can be proactively assigned a contingency reserve; when it is

unknown, a management reserve can be assigned for when it becomes an issue. The
objectives of project risk management are to increase the likelihood and impact of posi-

The project procurement management area includes one process in every phase with the
exception of the initiating phase. It includes processes for documenting project procure-

The project stakeholder management knowledge area includes one process in every
phase with the exception of the closing phase. It includes processes for identifying, man-
aging, engaging, and controlling people, groups, and organizations that could affect or be
affected by the project. Anyone who interacts with stakeholders should have the skills and
knowledge for analyzing their expectations and engaging them in project decisions and
execution. The processes here focus on continuous communication with stakeholders to
understand their needs and expectations, addressing issues as they occur, managing con-
flicting interests, and fostering their engagement when needed.
6.2 Prince2
A rival to PMBOK with widespread popularity in Europe and many other parts of the world
is PRINCE2. The acronym stands for “Projects in Controlled Environments” which is the de
facto choice of the UK government. Its latest version, PRINCE2 was published in 1996 and
includes contributions from 150 European organizations. Its key features include a focus
on business justification, a defined organizational structure for the project management
team, a product-based approach to planning with flexibility at a level appropriate to the
project, and an emphasis on dividing the project into manageable and controllable stages.
The various roles are distributed across four levels of management in PRINCE2 with corpo-
rate/program/management at the top instigating the project. Below them at the directing
level is the project board: the main decision-making body of the project. Then comes the
project manager at the managing level who manages the project on a daily basis; finally,
at the bottom in the delivering level are the team leaders and their teams who create the
project’s solution.
Like PMBOK, PRINCE2 is a process-based methodology that ensures project success based
on seven principles, seven themes, and seven processes. The seven principles are the
guiding requirements and good practices.

1.
Continued business justification: There should be a justifiable and documented rea-
son for running and managing the project. This principle ensures that the justification
is reviewed and updated throughout the project life cycle so that any progress can be
measured against its latest version.
2.
Focus on products: What the project creates in every step needs to be clearly defined
and agreed upon by its stakeholders. Product specifications are set out in the product
description that forms the basis of planning project activities, managing changes, and
verifying approval and acceptance of the product.
3.
Manage by stages: The project is divided by the project management team into a
number of time periods called “stages.” The duration of each stage can vary but the
project moves from one stage to the next after its outcome has been reviewed and
approved by the approval authority of the stage.
4.
Manage by exception: Each management level manages the level below using the
manage by exception principle. This means that each level delegates authority to
manage the project to the one below, which in turn reports to their level above once
they have successfully finished the work or when issues arise that breach the bounda-
ries of their delegated authority. Typically, the latter is done when deviations from the
project constraints (scope, schedule, etc.) are observed or anticipated.
5.
Defined roles and responsibilities: Each role in the project management team is
clearly understood by the person who undertakes that role. This includes roles for the
various management levels and stakeholders with various perspectives (user, sup-
plier, etc.). These roles and their responsibilities are first set forward in the project
brief and then in the project initiation documentation.
6.
Tailor to suit the project environment: Because no one project is like another, there is
no one-size-fits-all recipe for success, so the operational environment of the project
should dictate the specifics of each project. Many aspects should be considered,
including project size, complexity, importance, capability, and risk.
7.
Learn from experience: Mistakes and good practices from past projects are taken into
account when facing the challenges and threats of new projects. Lessons learned from
a project are disseminated so future projects and their teams can benefit from best
practices and avoid making past mistakes. A “lessons log” is typically used to record
previous and current experience.
Upon these seven principles, PRINCE2 builds seven themes that provide recommenda-
tions for carrying out various aspects of project management.
1.
Business case: This describes how to create and maintain a record of the business jus-
tification for the project and which roles are responsible for doing so. This theme
addresses the need for the development of a business case and highlights how the
benefits of the project can stay on track with stakeholder expectations. A benefits
review plan is one product of this theme (along with the business case), which speci-
fies when, how, and who will be involved with project reviews.
2.
Organization: This defines the individual roles and responsibilities of the project
team. This theme ensures that everyone understands their role in the project regard-
less of their level and perspectives (business, user, supplier). The interests of the busi-
ness, user, and supplier groups are represented in the main decision-making body of

the project: the project board. One key product of this theme is the communication
management strategy that defines how communications will be managed between
the various project stakeholders.
3.
Quality: This describes what the quality requirements and measures are, and how the
project will meet them. Managing quality is ensured by four components, including a
“quality system” that defines the approach to quality; the “quality assurance” func-
tion that checks whether the quality system is being implemented; “quality planning”
that defines the specifications of the project products; and “quality control” with
activities for verifying product specifications. A quality management strategy defines
how the four aspects of quality management will be approached.
4.
Plans: This describes the steps required to develop the plans, what the products will
be, the activities and the techniques that should be used to build those products, and
who will be assigned to each activity. The outcome of this theme is a project plan that
offers an overview of the project activities. This will serve as the basis for monitoring
project work via the project board. Additionally, there will be plans for each individual
stage of the project to help the project manager control the day-to-day activities, and
finally there will be the team plan with its highest level of detail. Often, an “exceptions
plan” is also developed to show how the project will recover from a breach in toleran-
ces.
5.
Risk: This describes the need to effectively identify risks and opportunities that could
impact the project. The five steps of identify, assess, plan, implement, and communi-
cate form the process for identifying and dealing with risk. With respect to threats,
one can avoid them so they do not occur, reduce them to minimize their impact, plan
actions for their mitigation, transfer them to a third party (like in the case of outsourc-
ing risky activities to more experienced entities), or make a conscious decision to
accept them. Assigning a “risk owner” who will monitor, manage, and control that risk
is part of this process.
6.
Change: This describes how the project manager will control and manage changes to
the project. This theme ensures that the right level of authority approves changes and
that configuration management helps to identify, monitor, and protect the project’s
product. Typical project issues that could trigger change are deviations or missing
specifications, violations of the project constraints such as cost or schedule, and lack
of support for the project. A change authority (individual or group) is usually designa-
ted to authorize change through a change control procedure. The latter is documen-
ted in the “configuration management strategy document” and includes activities for
capturing and documenting an issue, examining the issue and considering its impact
on the project, identifying solutions to alleviate the issue, deciding what the optimal
solution is, and implementing the decision.
7.
Progress: This covers the various ways of controlling activities, resources, and stake-
holders to ensure the project’s objectives are achieved. This is done by observing
deviations in tolerances of cost and time from their planed estimates. Tolerances are
specific to the organizational levels of the project, with the corporate and program
levels setting tolerances for the project board, which in turn sets tolerances for the
project manager who then sets work package tolerances for the team. Quality toleran-
ces (for product specifications) are established either at the project or product level,
while benefit tolerances (benefits for the project owner organization) are set only at

Based on the principles and themes, PRINCE2 establishes seven processes to showcase
the activities required to manage the project. They cover the management work from the
conception of the project idea to the end when the project solution is materialized and
accepted by the customer and the project resources are released. The roles of each project
entity/actor are described for each process as well as the management documents that
will be created, reviewed, or updated.

The seven processes are:
• Starting up a project: This covers work for investigating the feasibility of the project
through the development of the project concept. In this process, we design and appoint
the project management team and ensure that the required information is available. A
plan is also created for the next stage.

• Directing a project: This covers the activities of the project board in deciding whether
the project should start, when to transition to the next stage, and whether to close. This
process does not cover day-to-day activities—these are the domain of the project man-
ager—but provides a rather ad hoc direction by monitoring progress, providing advice
and guidance, and reacting to exception situations.
• Initiating a project: This covers the early planning activities (development of the project
plan). The existence of the foundations of the project and the business case are docu-
mented and confirmed while the project board is encouraged to take ownership of the
project. Agreeing to the commitment of resources for the first stage of the project
ensures the appropriate investment of time and effort will be made (taking potential
risks to the project into account).
• Controlling a stage of a project: This covers the daily management of the project by the
project manager and their proxies. This includes reporting, addressing issues and risk,
and delegating work. It also involves gathering progress information about the work,
watching for exceptions, and taking any necessary corrective actions.
• Managing product delivery: This covers work for developing the project solution,
including activities for creating work, accepting it, delivering it, and reporting progress.
The objective of this phase is to make sure work is effectively authorized, deliverables
conform to requirements, work progress is assessed and forecast regularly, and appro-
val is obtained for the completed products.
• Managing a stage boundary: This covers the work of the project manager at the end of
each major stage of the project, including reporting on the status and achievements of
the last stage and the plans for the next stage. Exceptions are addressed through this
process.
• Closing a project: This covers the work of the project manager to prepare for the end of
the project. This includes the end project report, archiving the project documents, and
handing the product over to the customer or the operational teams (if the product
needs to move on to manufacturing).
Compared to PMBOK, which is considered over-detailed (especially for small projects),
PRINCE2 is broader and higher-level with a well laid out and standardized approach that
dictates the roles and responsibilities of everyone in the project team. The common
vocabulary that PRINCE2 provides makes it easier to communicate reports, plans, and
other management documents between diverse stakeholders and to provide focus for
what needs to be done. The management-by-exception approach also ensures the effi-
cient and economic use of time and provides justification for undertaking the project at
all. Lessons learned can be disseminated and management assets can be reused, allowing
a resource continuation and the buildup of experience.
6.3 Multi-Project Management
In modern organizations, multiple projects are typically executed or planned concurrently
to address the various needs of the organization, from product and services development
to upgrades and maintenance of its infrastructure to exploring and implementing innova-
ted ideas and processes. Running multiple projects independently of each other will
exhaust resources and limit the organization’s ability to respond to market needs and to
128


=== PAGE 131 ===
grow (Miller & Hobbs, 2005). To address such issues, enterprises (especially the large ones)
organize their project activities under a portfolio umbrella in order to manage them in
groups under similar strategic objectives. Projects relate to each other in portfolios
through a common outcome of collective capability including sharing clients, technology,
staff, expertise, and other resources. That doesn’t necessarily mean the project shares
resources or characteristics (although this is typically the case). They could also be part of
a general organizational strategic initiative, for example, maximizing return on investment
or sustainable development. Centralizing project management as a portfolio ensures
alignment with organizational strategies and prioritization of resource allocation under
one command and control structure (Blichfeldt & Eskerod, 2008). This allows organiza-
tions to have an overall view of how the strategic goals are reflected in the portfolio, insti-
tute appropriate governance management, and authorize human, financial, or material
resources to be allocated based on expected performance and benefits.
Internally, in portfolios, projects are grouped into programs or subprograms based on
interdependencies in terms of resources, the organization’s strategic direction, and shared
governance structure. Organizing projects this way helps to manage them as a group and
in a coordinated fashion, align them with higher level strategies when they serve similar
goals and objectives, and share resources between them. Programs may also include ele-
ments of related work outside the scope of the discrete projects in the program.

is measured at the portfolio level in terms of the aggregate investment performance and
benefits that the portfolio produces, while at the program level it is measured by the
degree to which the program satisfies its specific need. At the lower levels, success is
measured by the extent to which the project meets the constraints and satisfies its stake-
holders.
In large organizations, the structure we discussed here is often under the project manage-
ment office (PMO) unit (Dai & Wells, 2004). This allows the standardization of project-rela-
ted governance processes and the sharing of resources, methodologies, tools, and techni-
ques. The unit reports to the executive level of the organization and feeds data to
performance systems like the balanced scorecard. As the overseeing authority for all
project activity in the organization, the PMO has the power to act as a project stakeholder
and interfere with project-related decisions throughout the life cycle of projects, including
making recommendations and even terminating projects that don’t align with the busi-
ness objectives of the organization.
Typical PMO structures range (based on the control and influence they exert) from suppor-
tive to controlling to directing (Martin, Pearson, & Furumo, 2005b). Supportive PMOs pro-
vide a consultative role to projects by supplying templates, best practices, training, access
to information, and lessons learned from other projects. This type of PMO serves as a
project repository and exerts low control over project activities and decisions. Controlling
PMOs provide support and require compliance through various means. Compliance may
involve adopting project management frameworks or methodologies, using specific tem-
plates, forms, and tools, or conforming to governance while exercising moderate control
over the portfolios. Directive PMOs, finally, take control by directly managing the projects
and all their details.

Regardless of their structure, the primary functions of PMOs are to manage shared resour-
ces across all the projects they administer; to identify and develop project management
methodologies, best practices, and standards; to provide coaching, mentoring, training,
and oversight; and to monitor compliance with project management standards, policies,
procedures, and templates by means of project audits (Hill, 2004). They are also responsi-
ble for developing and managing project policies, procedures, templates, and other
shared documentation (organizational process assets) and for coordinating communica-
tion across projects.
Organizations can facilitate the alignment of portfolio, program, and project management
activities by adapting their structure, culture, and processes and by optimizing the use of
their resources. Supplementing these alignment efforts with business impact analysis and
robust organizational enablers, organizations can flexibly move within their portfolio, pro-
gram, and project domains to maximize performance and realize business value (Hobbs,
Aubry, & Thuillier, 2008). This process begins with comprehensive strategic planning and
management guided by the organization’s vision and mission within the context of the
market environment where it operates.
6.4 Agile Software Processes in Large
Projects
While agile methodologies were initially developed for small teams, their popularity and
rates of success have led larger project teams to consider tailored versions of them for
their needs (Dingsøyr & Moe, 2013). By large projects, here we mean projects that involve
more than one independently operating team that could be co-located, distributed across
geographical locations, or virtual. Sometimes the term is used to indicate software
projects with large numbers of code and staff, large organizations, and/or a large number
of activities. While all these conditions could under certain circumstances suggest a large
project, the complexity of operating more than one team covers the challenges that large
projects face.
Implementing agile methodologites for large projects could benefit organizations as their
customer focus might result in increased customer satisfaction, rapid delivery, reduced
cost and overhead, and the development of products and services that address changing
market needs. However, applying agile methodologies in large projects is not so straight-
forward as in the small projects for which most of these methodologies were developed
(Dingsøyr & Moe, 2013). Scaling agile principles and practices poses many challenges that
stem from the longer planning horizons of large projects, the levels of authority that need
to be delegated, and the synchronization of deliverables, among others. Large projects
typically spread into more than a year, requiring product-to-market roadmaps of 12 to 18
months, while project team backlogs get refined at much shorter periods (two to three
weeks). This creates a disconnect in the level of refinement of the solution as the develop-
ment teams will work in details and finalize coding before they get the change requests
that the higher level will not identify until later. A lot of work-months can be lost in this
way, doing work that might be later be deemed unnecessary.

(Scrum) teams in order to coordinate their work.
Scrum of Scrums is a Scrum variant that consists of dividing a project team into groups of
agile teams (less than 12 individuals) and having each team elect an “ambassador” to par-
ticipate in a daily meeting with ambassadors from the rest of the teams (Stephens, 2015).
Typically, the Scrum master of each individual team plays that role, although technically
specialized team members can sometimes take their place. The meetings are short
(around 15 minutes long) and can be planned to occur as frequently as the team decides
(every other day or even weekly).

Large-scale Scrum (LeSS) is another variant of Scrum that resulted from efforts to apply
the purpose, elements, and elegance of Scrum to large projects as simply as possible.
Because such scaling requires deep organizational changes, LeSS is better seen as an
organizational design framework than a project management practice. Many of the princi-
ples and ideas of Scrum are still included, like a single product backlog, one definition of
done for all teams, one product owner, one sprint, one increment at the end of each
Scrum, and cross-functional teams. What is new is the sprint planning in part 1 and part 2
where part 1 plays the role of the typical team Scrum while part 2 is similar to the SoS we
saw before.
LeSS differs from SoS in that it sees the large-scale view as a single Scrum following the
principles, rules, elements, and purpose of Scrum—not as another level on top of Scrums.
It has nine core principles.
• “Transparency” is evident from tangible “done” items, short cycles, working together,
common definitions, and removing fear from the workplace.
• “More with less” suggests more responsibility with less rules, more customer-focus with
less artifacts, more team ownership of processes, and more meaningful work by having
less defined processes.
• “Whole-product focus” means one product backlog, one product owner, one shippable
product, and one sprint, regardless of the number of teams that are involved. This is
based on the fact that customers want valuable functionality in a cohesive product, not
technical components in separate parts.
• “Customer-centric” involves learning the customers’ real problems and solving them by
involving customers in meaningful feedback loops.
• “Continuous improvement” means pushing toward perfection through endless humble
and radical improvement experiments.
• “Lean thinking” means building an organizational system with a focus on eliminating
waste, for example by reducing partially done work and delivering results as fast as pos-
sible. At the same time, decisions are taken as late as possible to reduce uncertainty
and take account of the continuously changing environment.
• “Systems thinking” means understanding and optimizing the whole system (not parts)
and using systems modeling to explore system dynamics.
• “Empirical process control” means continually inspecting and adapting the product,
processes, behaviors, organizational design, and practices to evolve in situationally
appropriate ways instead of following prescribed sets of so-called “best practices” that
ignore context, create ritualistic following, impede learning and change, and squash
people’s sense of engagement and ownership.
• “Queuing theory” is applied by understanding how systems with queues behave in the
R&D domain and applying those insights to managing queue sizes, work-in-progress
limits, multitasking, work-packages, and variability.
SAFe is another approach to scaling agile across an enterprise that takes the portfolio,
program, and project hierarchy into consideration (Stephens, 2015). It aims at addressing
some of the problems with other scaled-up agile methodologies by decentralizing deci-
sion-making, taking an economic view, and applying systems thinking. Still at its core (as
for small projects) are the values of assuming variability and change and incremental
building with integrated learning cycles.

SAFe 4.6 introduced five core competencies that are required in lean enterprises: lean-
agile leadership, team and technical agility, DevOps and release on demand, business sol-
utions and lean systems engineering, and lean portfolio management. While each compe-
tency (with the exception of lean-agile leadership) targets different levels in the hierarchy
of project, program, and portfolio management, mastering each is critical to achieving
and sustaining a competitive advantage in today’s marketplace. In addition to the core
competencies, this new version includes new government guidance, which describes a set
of success patterns that help public sector organizations implement lean-agile practices.
The first competency (lean-agile leadership) describes how leaders drive organizational
change by empowering teams to reach their full potential. The leaders themselves model
lean-agile values, principles, and practices by exercising lifelong learning, teaching, and
coaching behaviors. They are the ones with the authority and power to change and
improve the system that governs how work is done, allowing the benefits of team agility to
add value. They also ensure the sustainable growth of their organization, their teams, and
themselves as leaders and professionals.
At the team member level, individuals need to adhere to lean-agile principles and be tech-
nically competent to deliver high-quality solutions that support customer requirements
and future business needs. Team members succeed or fail together as they are accounta-
ble and committed to common goals. One primary goal is to deliver quality solutions in
the fastest possible time. As a result, testing plays a core role in such teams and is imple-
mented at the feature level, the story level, and the code level. As soon as a feature is com-
pleted and tested at the code level, it is then tested at the story level, and then at the fea-
ture level. This test-first mindset ensures a flow from code quality, to system quality, to
architecture and design quality, and finally to release quality.
The DevOps and release-on-demand competencies apply to the program level and
describe how the principles of development (Dev) and operations (Ops) can shorten the
SDLC while aligning to business objectives. This allows organizations to learn and adjust
quickly to address market needs through rapid and frequent delivery. Organization mem-
bers like product managers, system architects, etc. need to release value on demand and
continuously work together to optimize the full flow of value. Such groups of cross-func-
tional agile teams are considered to be aboard the agile release train (ART). Each ART runs
across the continuous delivery pipeline, improving the flow of value and enabling release
on demand through continuous exploration, continuous integration, and continuous
deployment. This is grounded in the five concepts of culture, automation, lean flow, meas-
urement, and recovery (CALMR).
• “Culture” represents the philosophy of shared responsibility for fast value delivery
across the entire value stream, including product management, development, testing,
security, compliance, and operations.
• “Automation” represents the work and assets needed to remove human intervention
from as much of the pipeline as possible. This shortens time-to-market and improves
quality.
• “Lean flow” covers the concepts of limiting WIP, reducing batch size, and managing
queue lengths to enable faster flow through the pipeline.

=== PAGE 138 ===
• “Measurement” is about understanding and measuring the flow of value through the
pipeline, thereby fostering learning and continuous improvement.
• “Recovery” focuses on building systems that allow fast fixes of production issues
through automatic rollback and “fix forward” capabilities.
To interface between the program and portfolio levels requires lean system competency.
This enables the collaboration and contribution of large and diverse numbers of actors
(developers, suppliers, customer, etc.) who might have compliance concerns and interests
that demand a broad range of multidisciplinary skills. To allow for continual development,
SAFe replaces the traditional approach to executing projects in phases with a flow-based
process that uses roadmaps for continuous integration. A set of practices that inform solu-
tion-builders include:
• building solutions components with high-functioning ARTs. Capabilities and compo-
nents of the system are built by ARTs, which synchronize work by operating with a regu-
lar tact called cadence for planning, demonstrating, improving, and learning.
• building and integrating with a solution train. Solution trains integrate the work of the
ARTs and implement the flow of value needed to deliver the full system.
• capturing and refining systems specifications in the solution intent. The solution intent
repository manages and provides detailed information about the intent of the solution
—nonfunctional requirements (NFRs), interface definitions, system decompositions,
assignments of requirements to different ARTs, architectural guidelines, and anything
else needed to keep teams aligned with the vision. SAFe also provides the flexibility to
“assume variability and preserve options,” allowing some requirements to be fixed
while others remain open and variable.
• applying multiple planning horizons. ARTs and teams use backlogs and roadmaps to
manage work and forecast the schedule. Applying the principle “plan to what you
know,” teams develop detailed plans for the near term and less detailed plans for the
long term.
• architecting for scale, modularity, reusability, and serviceability. SAFe uses principles
like intentional architecture, emergent design, modularity, and service-based design to
provide for an evolving and flexible architecture. This approach avoids constraining
innovation by defining too many details up front.
• managing the supply chain with “systems of systems” thinking. Suppliers of strategic
components become part of a virtual ART, participating in planning, demonstration,
integration, and continuous improvement activities.
• applying continuous integration. SAFe strikes a balance between the desire to integrate
as frequently as possible and the cost of integrating the entire system. This decision is
an economic trade-off between the cost and value of integrating frequently—including
the insights that it generates.
• continually addressing compliance concerns. Large solutions are often subject to strict
compliance requirements. ARTs and teams include compliance testing, documentation,
and other mandated requirements in their backlogs, and continuously apply definitions
of done, test automation, and other activities.
The last of the core competences—lean portfolio management (LPM)—addresses the high-
est level of the project portfolios. This competency describes how an organization can
implement lean approaches to strategy and investment funding, agile portfolio opera-

tions, and lean governance. These value streams allow an organization to set practices
that help meet its business strategy either internally by supporting its business process or
externally by adding value to the solutions they provide to customers. Mastering this com-
petency allows for a transition from traditional practices (like rigid annual planning and
budgeting, overhead of demand over capacity , and centralizing requirements in the
hands of people who are not involved in the solution development) to executing commit-
ments reliably and enabling innovation across the organization.
At the highest level of the portfolio, SAFe uses lean principles like the prioritization of
“epics” based on the optimization of value streams that could be further broken down at
the program level. Epics capture and manage the most significant initiatives within a port-
folio. They can be distinguished into “business epics” for large customer-facing initiatives
that encapsulate developments that target new business opportunities and “enabler
epics” that are used to advance the architectural runway to support the business epics.
Further down the hierarchy, epics can be split into “program” and “solution” epics to facil-
itate incremental implementation. An “epics owner” is typically responsible for their sta-
tus and liaises with the lower levels to provide clarification and ensure understanding.
At the program level, SAFe applies Scrum in principle but with longer sprint cycles where
the last cycle (often called the “innovation planning sprint”) is dedicated to innovation,
inspection, and adaptation. This is where the ART is formed by groups of 8–12 in a team
that performs long-term development based on the epics and the organization’s strategy.
This level pulls from the backlog of business and architectural epics and splits them into
features that the teams within the ART will implement through 8–12-week iterative cycles.
This provides cadences and synchronization and forces all teams to be in the same sprint
schedule to reduce risk and internal dependencies and instill a common sense of purpose
and shared ownership.
At the lower levels of the project, SAFe typically adopts Scrum with all its roles, processes,
and artifacts. Team requirements are managed by the product owners, are a subset of the
program backlog, and are stored in the team backlog. The cross-functional teams are
expected to independently self-organize and complete requirements within the ART’s
cadence. Scrum does not imply engineering practices; rather, “extreme programming” is
used to guide development. Practices such as continuous integration and test-driven
development can and should be implemented to ensure scalability and quality.
The biggest benefit of adopting SAFe is that it combines the flexibility of a lightweight
framework with centralized decision-making at the enterprise level (Stephens, 2015). It
essentially connects project teams with the higher-level strategies of their organizations
and ensures that the strategic objectives of the organization are seen and take priority
over everything else. Additionally, the standardization it offers allows small teams to com-
municate through a common language and efficiently coordinate their work. Some of its
drawbacks are that it is often seen as enforcing a top-down approach to decision-making
and creates overhead due to the additional layers of oversight, administration, and coor-
dination it imposes. The emphasis it places on the big picture can sometimes lead to lon-
ger planning cycles and more fixed roles within development cycles. Front-line actors like

products or services they develop are often excluded from the decision-making process at

6.5 Selection of the Appropriate Project
Management Method
Choosing a project management methodology can be a challenge for new and experi-
enced project managers alike. It should be evident from all we have seen so far that a one-
size-fits-all methodology is not a reality for project managers in the IT field. In fact, such
an attitude is more of a counterproductive liability that rarely supports organizational
maturity and growth. Off-the-shelf methodologies can be an easy answer from a manage-
ment perspective since they are a “simple” matter of paying licensing fees, getting people
accredited, and organizing the workplace with supporting material. Such decisions are
based, though, in the faulty assumption that the methodology is right for the particular
organization, that teams will actually use it, and that it will ensure and accelerate success-
ful project delivery. Tailor-made methodologies might be the right answer—if the organi-
zation is willing to put in the time and resources—as the results will be longer lasting and
customized to the organization (Vijayasarathy & Butler, 2016).
Choosing a methodology can be affected by multiple internal and external factors, includ-
ing the organizational structure and culture of the company (Harkiolakis, 2016). Organiza-
tional structure emerges through role distribution and the breakdown of organizational
activities into functions that address specific needs. Hierarchies and operating procedures
enforce and adapt such structures as the organization interacts with its environment.
Functional structures typically support the breakdown into specializations that contribute
to each project as needed and for as long as a project is running, while matrix structures
organize employees in dual reporting relationships by both function and product/service
or project. Due to its focus on functions, functional structures have simplified manage-
ment control, better division of labor, economies of scale, and efficiency in function execu-
tion. With respect to projects and especially when cross-functionality is needed, the lack
of coordination, control, and accountability of multifunctional activities can be detrimen-
tal to projects. This is aggravated in cases where functional units operate as silos, allowing
friction between them to degrade operational performance and cost the organization in
maintenance and product/service efficiency.
Matrix structures, on the other hand, allow for good coordination across functions or divi-
sion, and aim at quickly responding to market needs by expanding on and leveraging
employees’ competencies. By allowing specialists within an organization to be used in a
number of project teams, the organization makes effective use of its resources—in particu-
lar, the best deployment of those skills that are in short supply. The transient nature of
project teams can be demotivating in such structures and can lead to an imbalance or fric-
tion between the sides of the matrix, so the approach requires clear coordination and
management to be effective. As project teams are created and dissolved frequently, one
major concern with any structure that also affects whatever project management method-

=== PAGE 141 ===
ology the organization and the team adopts is its alignment with the organizational mis-
sion and vision. Team loyalty builds within the project, but after the end of the project,
team members return to the organizational pool or are let go.
Organizational culture is another core element to consider when selecting a model for
project management—among the first attempts at theoretically framing this quality are
the structural and interpretive views of culture. The structural view focuses on how roles
are structured in an organization and has created a categorization of cultures into “power
cultures” (usually observed in small organizations) where communication is informal and
in compliance with the attitudes and norms of the central power source; “role cultures”
(usually observed in larger bureaucratic organizations) where coordination is provided by
the elite and clear roles for employees are reflected in their job description; “task cultures”
(usually observed in matrix-type organizations) where power lies at the intersection of
roles; and “person cultures” (usually observed in professional organizations) where the
individual is the key element. The interpretive view, on the other hand, adopts a strategic
outlook and focuses on how the various design features (like culture and structure) and
human qualities conspire to enable managers to make sense of the world in a restricted/
specialized way. Regardless of the view one adopts, culture is expressed through formal
statements like the organizational mission and vision: The former is a statement that
describes the reason for the organization’s existence while the latter shows where the
organization wishes to be. Along with organizational structure, culture has a spillover
effect on projects and drastically affects how projects are organized and perform.
Based on the way the various models handle project requirements, we can distinguish
between them as predictive or adaptive (Stephens, 2015). In predictive models, we antici-
pate the next steps and plan accordingly ahead of an activity; in adaptive models, we con-
sider change as part of the core and follow processes that are flexible and can adapt to
changes as they happen. The key advantage of predictive models is that they allow for
accurate planning and organization of resources ahead of time in a controlled and predict-
able way, whereas the adaptive models allow for fast responses to changing or unclear
requirements. The former is ideal for well-structured or short duration projects where not
much change is expected and in cases where we cannot afford to experiment with require-
ments—in particular, complex systems including hardware as well as software (and strong
interdependencies between the two) cannot afford to change requirements too often
because hardware changes tend to be more costly and take a longer time. For example, if
we are building the information technology needed for a space shuttle, it is unlikely that
we can afford to change requirements halfway through its development, so a predictive
model where requirements are exhaustively gathered at the beginning would be more
beneficial. On the other hand, if we are developing a computer game, we might need to go
back and forth as we get feedback about its performance from the users—suggesting that
an adaptive model would work better. In general, if our resources are limited and/or if the
requirements are incomplete, unclear, or expected to change, we are better off with an
adaptive methodology; otherwise, a predictive model may be the better choice.
For adaptive models, a further breakdown into iterative and agile can be considered. Itera-
tive models provide all the features required in the solution at a low but usable fidelity
(level of completeness) from the earliest iterations and increase the fidelity of the features
until the solution addresses all the required features at full fidelity. Agile models, on the

other hand, start with the fewest features at low fidelity (but functional) and keep refining
and adding features progressively through iterations until all the required features are at
full fidelity. Between the two, we often find another category of incremental models where
the fewest possible features at full fidelity are addressed first and later iterations target
additional features (again at full fidelity) until all the required features are implemented at
full fidelity.

exactly what is expected of every phase. The project constraints will be met and any
issues, whether anticipated or not, will be dealt with early on, ensuring that a quality solu-

inflexible nature—it will not allow for changes, even if they are opportunities that could
improve processes and performance.
In the agile category of methodologies, we saw Scrum and SAFe, among others. As in most
other cases, each of these methodologies can be adapted and customized to address the
needs of different development teams. While this might be a disadvantage for other meth-
odologies, customization lies at the heart of agile development. This is more necessary
than anything else because each project is different and consequently different tools
(even if only slightly different) should be expected to work best for each project. What they

Regardless of the choice between an existing model and a customized version, organiza-
tional and project leadership needs to ensure everyone on the project team is aware of,
and understands, the specifics of the model, they all agree on what rules and procedures
will be followed, and they are all committed to following them. Eventually, what really
matters is that projects deliver high-quality solutions within the agreed-upon constraints
(schedule, cost, etc.) whether they are products or services.
SUMMARY
In large projects, there are more dependencies between different parts
of the project and its environment, and a more thorough approach to
planning is needed as a result. Nevertheless, efforts have been made to
scale agile project management to larger projects, essentially splitting
the work into small agile teams and adding additional levels for coordi-
nation of these teams and the work they perform. Well-known method-
ologies for this purpose are “Scrum of Scrums” and the scaled agile
framework (SAFe).
There are two widely known standardized approaches to project man-
agement which are mainly suited to large projects. The project manage-
ment institute (PMI) publishes the “project management body of knowl-
edge (PMBOK),” the goal of which is to describe the knowledge needed
for project management. This knowledge is described in 47 processes,
grouped into 10 knowledge areas, and assigned to five project process
groups roughly describing the main phases: initiating, planning, execut-
ing, monitoring and controlling, and closing.
The second widely known approach to project management is PRINCE2
and was published on behalf of the UK government. It describes similar
contents to the PMBOK but is somewhat less formal.
Depending on the project environment and the results to be created, an
adequate methodology must be selected for each project. This may be
an agile approach, a plan-driven approach, or, more often, some combi-
nation of the two.
