2. COMPLETE SUMMARY NOTES - INTRODUCTION & 5.1 RUP
Small and medium project context
• Small projects may have only a few developers or even one developer; people often perform several roles.
• Less documentation can be practical because oral communication is easier, but small teams may have fewer resources, weaker formal 
quality processes and less specialist knowledge.
• Individual accountability and dependency are high because a single member may be difficult to replace.
• Small teams can communicate efficiently, but small size does not remove the need for disciplined project management.
Unified Process (UP) and RUP
• UP is iterative and incremental. It is customizable and organizes work across life-cycle phases while engineering processes occur with 
different intensity in each phase.
Phase
What to memorize
Inception
Turn a business need into a project idea. Outline goals, initial schedule, 
key requirements/constraints/features, risks and the business case. Check 
stakeholder agreement and project credibility.
Elaboration
Develop/refine requirements and architecture without over-constraining 
design. Identify risks. Use UML/architectural views, use cases and class 
hierarchies where appropriate.
Construction
Program, test and debug the solution. Focus on thoroughly tested, high-
quality deliverables ready for user acceptance.
Transition
Transfer the solution to users/customer and maintenance. Address 
feedback, deployment/hardware needs, documentation and training; may 
iterate back if needed.
Optional: Production
Observe real use after initial completion and collect insight for future 
upgrades/related projects.
Optional: Disposal
Remove/replace the solution safely when users transition to a successor 
system.
RUP = Rational Unified Process
• RUP is the best-known UP variant; developed by Rational and later associated with IBM tooling.
• RUP keeps the four core phases but organizes work across six engineering disciplines: business modeling, requirements gathering, 
analysis and design, implementation, testing and deployment.
• The effort of each discipline changes by phase: business modeling is strongest early; deployment becomes strongest late. Multiple iterations 
can occur within phases.
RUP best practices
• Iterative development to handle changing user requirements.
• Component-based development so parts can be reused and independently tested before integration.
• UML/visual modeling to clarify components, interactions and actor roles.
• Continuous integration to reduce problems caused by different tools/configurations across teams.
• Testing is integrated throughout, although its intensity becomes greater toward later stages.
RUP advantages and limitation
• Advantage: IBM/Rational tools historically supplied templates, modeling, visualization, document management, change tracking and 
performance profiling.
• Limitation: strong dependence on these tools made RUP harder to apply without them; the course book notes this as one reason RUP is no 
longer widely used.

3. COMPLETE SUMMARY NOTES - 5.2 AGILE SOFTWARE PROCESSES
Prototyping before Agile
• Prototype = learning tool used early to reduce uncertainty, test a user interface/requirement or verify a technical concept.
• Throw-away prototype: discarded after the learning question is answered.
• Evolutionary prototype: repeatedly extended/revised until it becomes the product.
• Risk: a quickly built prototype may be mistaken for a production-ready solution even though integration/business rules may be incomplete.
How Agile differs from prototyping
• Each Agile iteration should deliver functional software, not merely a learning mock-up.
• Agile is a philosophy/framework of values and behaviors: trust, flexibility, empowerment and collaboration.

Twelve Agile principles - exam-safe paraphrase
1. Deliver valuable software early and continuously to satisfy the customer.
2. Welcome changing requirements, even late, when they provide advantage.
3. Deliver working software frequently, preferring shorter cycles.
4. Businesspeople and developers collaborate throughout the project.
5. Build around motivated people; provide support and trust them.
6. Use direct, rich communication where practical.
7. Use working software as the primary progress measure.
8. Maintain a sustainable pace for sponsors, developers and users.
9. Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design improves agility.
10. Practice simplicity: avoid unnecessary work.
11. Let strong architectures, requirements and designs emerge from self-organizing teams.
12. Regularly reflect, learn and adjust how the team works.
Self-organizing teams
• Have authority and flexibility to decide how to accomplish the work.
• Members monitor their own progress, take responsibility and seek support when needed.
• They are the opposite of micromanaged teams.
• Spontaneous self-organization is difficult; inspired leadership should encourage initiative and support rather than control every action.
• Over time, collaborative responsibility/accountability structures emerge.
Communication, quality and iterations
• Frequent customer/proxy communication keeps the work aligned with expectations.
• Customer feedback at the end of iterations drives changes in the next iteration.
• Quality depends on good coding principles, common practices/style and technical excellence.
• Iterations are short and contain analysis, design, coding, testing and verification.
• At iteration end, customer feedback supports a go/no-go decision when appropriate.
• Goal of an iteration: a working system the user can validate.

4. COMPLETE SUMMARY NOTES - 5.3 SCRUM
What Scrum is
• Scrum is an Agile framework built around self-organizing teamwork, fixed time/cost and flexible requirements.
• It uses timeboxes, collaborative events, a prioritized product backlog and frequent feedback cycles.
• The Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland defines its roles, events, artifacts and rules.
• Scrum is empirical: knowledge comes from experience and repeated inspection/adaptation.
Three pillars
Pillar
Meaning
Transparency
Important process aspects are visible and understood consistently.
Inspection
Artifacts/progress are checked often enough to detect undesirable 
deviations.
Adaptation
When deviations exceed acceptable limits, the process/product is 
adjusted.
Five Scrum values
• Commitment • Courage • Focus • Openness • Respect.
Three Scrum roles
Role
Key responsibility
Product Owner
Customer/user/stakeholder proxy. Owns and prioritizes product backlog, 
communicates vision, clarifies requirements, verifies product needs, plans 
releases and keeps stakeholders informed.
Scrum Master
Servant/support role for the team. Removes obstacles, protects self-
organization, facilitates communication/meetings, mediates discussions, 
ensures good Scrum practice and challenges the team to improve.
Team Members
Egalitarian self-organizing development group jointly responsible for the 
solution; distribute work, estimate and report progress to each other.
• The course book describes a typical Scrum team as about five to nine individuals. Scrum does not use a traditional project manager role 
inside the self-organizing team.
Artifacts and flow
Concept
Memorize
Product Backlog
Prioritized list of desired product features; maintained by the Product 
Owner.
Sprint
Fixed-duration, time-boxed iteration; course book notes it normally 
does not exceed one month.
Sprint Backlog
Selected high-priority backlog items the team commits to address 
during the sprint.
Increment
Working solution produced/demonstrated at sprint end.
Task Board
Common supporting tool: To Do -> Doing -> Done (and optional 
feature/requirement columns).
Four Scrum events - memorize the sequence
Event
Purpose
Sprint Planning
Select priority product-backlog items for the sprint; create sprint backlog 
and plan how work will be done.
Daily Scrum
Short daily coordination/progress meeting; course book gives about 15 
minutes.
Sprint Review
Demonstrate the increment to stakeholders and collect feedback.
Sprint Retrospective
Reflect on team/process/product quality and identify improvements for the 
next sprint; may refine Definition of Done.
Release sprint and advantages/challenges
• When backlog features are finished, a release iteration/sprint may finalize documentation, defects, integration/testing and deployment 
materials, followed by final acceptance and shipping.
• Advantages: frequent feedback, visibility, transparency, empowerment/accountability, easier accommodation of change and faster issue 
resolution.
• Challenge: Scrum depends on team understanding and experience. Weak Scrum knowledge - especially weak facilitation - can damage the 
development process.
• Course-book recommendation: preserve experienced Scrum teams rather than repeatedly breaking them apart.

5. COMPLETE SUMMARY NOTES - 5.4 PLAN-DRIVEN METHODS
Why small projects still need discipline
• Small projects may have fewer people but still carry customer communication and other external dependencies.
• People may have overlapping responsibilities; strict quality standards may have to be achieved with fewer resources.
• Groupthink can cause small teams to over-focus on familiar life-cycle phases and neglect others.
Waterfall model
• Predictive/sequential: predict work in advance; each phase normally begins after the previous phase is completed.
• Typical course-book sequence: requirements gathering -> system analysis/design -> coding/development -> verification/testing -> 
deployment.
• The course book gives a rough allocation: 20-40% on requirements + analysis/design, 30-40% on coding/development, remainder on 
testing/deployment.
When Waterfall fits
• Short projects with stable requirements.
• Projects where reliability/quality is critical and strong planning is possible.
• Environments where stakeholders need clear progress visibility and up-front structure.
Waterfall advantages and weakness
• Advantages: stable plan, complete documentation, predictable scheduling/resource/cost allocation, easy progress monitoring.
• Main weakness: poor response to unclear/changing requirements; testing and customer validation may occur late, when change is 
expensive.

7. IMPORTANT SAQ - SHORT QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS
Q. Why can small projects still be difficult to manage?
Answer: They may have fewer resources, overlapping roles, limited specialist knowledge and high dependency on individual team members, 
while customer communication and quality obligations still remain.
Q. What does iterative development mean?
Answer: Work is repeated in cycles so results can be refined using feedback and new information.
Q. What does incremental development mean?
Answer: The product is built in smaller functional parts that are progressively added until the full solution emerges.
Q. Name the four core Unified Process phases.
Answer: Inception, elaboration, construction and transition.
Q. What happens in the inception phase?
Answer: A business need becomes a project idea; goals, initial schedule, core requirements, constraints, features, risks and the business case 
are outlined and project credibility is checked.
Q. What happens in elaboration?
Answer: Requirements and core architecture are refined, risks are analyzed and modeling such as UML/use cases/class structures may be 
developed.
Q. What happens in construction?
Answer: The system is programmed, tested and debugged to produce high-quality deliverables ready for acceptance.
Q. What happens in transition?
Answer: The system is transferred to users/customer and maintenance, with deployment, feedback, documentation, training and necessary 
adjustments.
Q. Name the six RUP engineering disciplines.
Answer: Business modeling, requirements, analysis and design, implementation, testing and deployment.
Q. Name the two prototype types in Unit 5.
Answer: Throw-away and evolutionary prototypes.
Q. State the four Agile values.
Answer: Individuals/interactions; working software; customer collaboration; responding to change - preferred over processes/tools, 
comprehensive documentation, contract negotiation and following a plan respectively.
Q. What is a self-organizing team?
Answer: A team with authority and flexibility to decide how to do the work, monitor its progress and take responsibility without 
micromanagement.
Q. What is the primary measure of progress in Agile?
Answer: Working software.
Q. Name the three Scrum pillars.
Answer: Transparency, inspection and adaptation.
Q. Name the five Scrum values.
Answer: Commitment, courage, focus, openness and respect.
Q. Name the three Scrum roles.
Answer: Product Owner, Scrum Master and Team Members/Development Team.
Q. Who owns the product backlog?
Answer: The Product Owner.
Q. What is a sprint?
Answer: A fixed-duration time-boxed iteration containing Scrum work/events and intended to produce a usable increment.
Q. Name the four Scrum events.
Answer: Sprint planning, daily Scrum, sprint review and sprint retrospective.
Q. What is the purpose of a sprint review?
Answer: Demonstrate the increment to stakeholders and obtain feedback.
Q. What is the purpose of a sprint retrospective?
Answer: Reflect on the team, process and quality, then identify improvements for the next sprint.
Q. What is the main weakness of Waterfall?
Answer: It handles changing or unclear requirements poorly, and important validation may occur late when change is costly.
Q. What is Waterfall with feedback?
Answer: A Waterfall variant that permits backward movement to earlier phases for correction.
Q. What is the Sashimi model?
Answer: A Waterfall variant in which phases overlap when dependencies permit.
Q. What is the V-model?
Answer: A predictive model pairing each development/decomposition level on the left with a corresponding integration/verification level on the 
right.
Q. When is a hybrid approach appropriate?
Answer: When a project needs both predictive control/reliability and iterative adaptation to changing requirements.

8. MOST IMPORTANT 6-MARK QUESTIONS - MODEL ANSWERS
Q. Explain the four core phases of the Rational Unified Process (RUP).
• Inception converts a business need into a credible project idea and outlines goals, constraints, risks and the business case.
• Elaboration refines requirements and architecture and reduces major risks.
• Construction develops, tests and debugs the solution.
• Transition delivers the solution to users/maintenance and includes deployment, documentation, training and feedback.
• RUP is iterative: phases can contain repeated cycles for refinement.
• Optional production/disposal phases may extend the full product life cycle.
Q. Explain the four Agile values and their practical meaning.
• Individuals and interactions are preferred to over-reliance on processes/tools.
• Working software is preferred to excessive documentation.
• Customer collaboration is preferred to treating the contract as the only interaction mechanism.
• Responding to change is preferred to rigidly following a plan.
• The values do not mean the items on the right are worthless; they set priority when trade-offs arise.
• Together they support flexibility, feedback and value delivery under changing requirements.
Q. Explain self-organizing teams in Agile.
• Self-organizing teams have authority and flexibility over how work is performed.
• Members distribute work and take responsibility for results.
• They monitor their own progress and seek help when needed.
• They are designed to reduce micromanagement and increase initiative/motivation.
• Success requires competent, motivated people and strong communication.
• Inspired leadership supports and removes obstacles rather than controlling every task.
Q. Explain Scrum pillars and values.
• Transparency makes important work/process information visible and commonly understood.
• Inspection checks artifacts/progress often enough to detect deviation.
• Adaptation changes the process/product when deviations exceed acceptable limits.
• The supporting values are commitment, courage, focus, openness and respect.
• The pillars create empirical control based on experience.
• The values make the pillars workable within a self-organizing team.
Q. Compare the Product Owner and Scrum Master.
• The Product Owner represents customers/users/stakeholders and owns the product backlog.
• The Product Owner communicates product vision, prioritizes requirements and plans/releases value.
• The Scrum Master supports the team and protects self-organization.
• The Scrum Master removes obstacles, facilitates communication/events and promotes good Scrum practice.
• The Product Owner focuses on what/value and priority; the Scrum Master focuses on process/team effectiveness.
• Neither role is equivalent to a traditional command-and-control project manager.
Q. Explain the four Scrum events.
• Sprint Planning selects priority backlog items and creates the sprint backlog/plan.
• Daily Scrum is a short daily coordination and progress review.
• Sprint Review demonstrates the increment to stakeholders and collects feedback.
• Sprint Retrospective examines how the team/process can improve before the next sprint.
• All operate inside a fixed-duration sprint/timebox.
• Together they create rapid feedback, inspection and adaptation.
Q. Compare Waterfall, Waterfall with feedback and Sashimi.
• Classic Waterfall is sequential; one phase normally finishes before the next begins.
• Waterfall with feedback permits backward movement to earlier phases.
• Long backward jumps can create major cost/schedule impact.
• Sashimi allows phases to overlap where dependencies permit.
• Overlap can accelerate work and improve resource utilization while retaining predictive structure.
• All remain more plan-driven than Agile and work best when requirements can be predicted reasonably well.
Q. Explain the V-model and its suitability.
• The V-model is a Waterfall-like predictive model shaped as a V.
• The left side decomposes requirements/design into increasingly detailed construction work.
• The right side integrates results and verifies them at matching abstraction levels.
• Each development level has a corresponding verification/testing level.
• It emphasizes traceability, correctness and systematic validation.
• It is particularly suitable where safety, security, reliability or regulated quality is critical.
Q. Compare Agile and plan-driven project management for small projects.
• Agile minimizes up-front detail and adapts through short iterations and customer feedback.
• Plan-driven methods define requirements/phases more fully before execution.

9. HIGH-YIELD 20-MARK CASE-STUDY BLUEPRINT
Use this structure when the question asks you to choose/manage a method for a small or medium software 
project.
1. Diagnose requirement stability: stable/regulated vs unclear/changing.
2. Assess project size, dependencies, customer access, safety/security/reliability needs and documentation burden.
3. If requirements evolve quickly, justify Agile: short iterations, working software, customer feedback, self-organizing team.
4. If Scrum is selected, define Product Owner, Scrum Master, team, product backlog, sprint backlog, increment and the four events.
5. If a structured iterative approach is preferred, explain RUP phases and six disciplines.
6. If reliability and fixed requirements dominate, justify Waterfall or V-model.
7. Use Waterfall-with-feedback or Sashimi when some correction/overlap is useful but predictive control is retained.
8. Compare strengths and limitations, not just definitions.
9. Explain quality/verification and stakeholder feedback throughout the chosen process.
10. Recommend a hybrid approach if the case has both regulated/stable components and uncertain/changeable components.
11. Close with a clear method selection and why it minimizes the project-specific risks.

• V-model for safety/security-critical software.

• Hybrid approach for a project with stable compliance requirements but changing user-interface features.
